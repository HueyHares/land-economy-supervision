1The puzzle

Currently, London produces 28.5% more per hour worked than the UK average, while the North East produces 14.6% less (ONS, 2025), highlighting a productivity gap between the North and South. Successive governments have sought to address this disparity through greater investment into transport projects, which aim to improve regional connectivity and reduce structural barriers to growth.

The two flagship schemes of the past fifteen years, however, target different transport costs. HS2 aimed to improve connections between London, Manchester and Leeds, before its northern extension was cancelled in October 2023, with funding redirected to “Network North”[HoC Library]. Northern Powerhouse Rail, revived in January 2026 with a £45bn funding cap, instead aims to improve connectivity between northern cities, linking Liverpool, Manchester, Leeds and York[NAO 2026].

Both schemes, on the surface, may appear to be justified on the assumption that cheaper movement increases connectivity between regions, which would result in less regional inequality. The puzzle is that this need not be true. A faster line between a large, productive core and a smaller periphery does not only let northern firms reach London's customers; it equally lets London's firms reach northern customers without having to locate there, and if the second effect dominates, the periphery can lose activity to the core. Whether a transport link reduces or increases regional inequality may therefore depend less on the amount invested and more on which regions it connects.

The question: do transport links reduce regional inequality, or deepen it through agglomeration?

2The theory

New Economic Geography, particularly Krugman's (1991) core–periphery model, explains when this occurs. When firms produce differentiated goods under increasing returns to scale this encourages them to locate in larger markets where they can reach more customers at lower transport costs. Workers are then attracted to these regions, as more firms mean a greater variety of goods and a lower cost of living (forward linkage). As more workers arrive, demand increases, attracting further firms (backward linkage) and workers, highlighting the effects of circular and cumulative causation (Myrdal, 1957). However, these agglomeration forces can be opposed by dispersion forces, as immobile workers maintain demand across regions, while overcrowding also raises the living costs in larger cities.

The balance between agglomeration and dispersion forces depends on the level of trade costs. The tomahawk diagram below illustrates the stable outcomes for two identical regions at different levels of trade costs (τ), where τ = 1.5 means that 1.5 units must be shipped for one unit to arrive. When trade costs are high, industry remains evenly distributed, as firms benefit from locating near their customers. However, once trade costs fall below the break point, the even split becomes unstable, as firms increasingly benefit from concentrating in one region, where they gain better access to customers and suppliers. This encourages mobile industry to cluster in one region. Introducing congestion costs changes this outcome, as higher living costs weaken the benefits of agglomeration. This extends Krugman's original model, which does not include congestion costs. At sufficiently low trade costs, firms can relocate away from the crowded core while still serving its market cheaply, allowing industry to become more dispersed.

3How the model works

The simulator is built on Krugman's (1991) core–periphery model, following the versions in Fujita, Krugman and Venables (1999, ch. 5) and Brakman, Garretsen and van Marrewijk (2019, ch. 7). The economy consists of three regions: London and the South East, the North West (Manchester), and Yorkshire and the Humber (Leeds). The workforce is divided into two groups: mobile and immobile workers. Mobile workers, who make up 30% of the workforce, are skilled workers employed in firms producing tradable goods and services, such as finance, technology and professional services. They move to whichever region offers the highest real wage, with firms following them. Immobile workers, who make up the remaining 70%, remain in their respective regions, ensuring that their spending maintains some demand in each region.

The key equation

Mobile workers compare regions using a single measure, the real wage:

ω = w · G−μ · N−δ
PartWhat it capturesEffect as a region grows
w · wageFirms with better access to customers in every region can pay more (backward linkage)Pulls workers in
G · price indexGoods are cheaper where more firms are nearby, as fewer goods carry transport costs (forward linkage)Pulls workers in
N · congestionCrowding raises rents and commuting costs, reducing living standardsPushes workers out

The first two parts are the agglomeration forces in Krugman's original model, and the third is a congestion cost added for this project, following Brakman et al. (1996). Transport costs (τ) enter through the first two: a cheaper link changes both where firms can sell and what consumers pay.

How it runs

The model cannot be solved by hand, so the simulator solves it numerically. It calculates each region's real wage, moves a small number of mobile workers towards the regions where it is highest, and then recalculates. This repeats until real wages are equal wherever mobile workers live, so that no one can gain by moving. This end point, the spatial equilibrium, is the result shown on the map.

Show the full equations

The wage and price index in the key equation are calculated from three supporting equations, which together form the short-run equilibrium of the model.

Yr = μλrwr + (1 − μ)φr

Income. A region's spending power is what its mobile workers earn plus what its immobile workers earn (with a wage fixed at 1).

Gr = [ Σs λs(wsTsr)1−σ ]1/(1−σ)

Price index. The average price of tradable goods in region r, which is lower where more firms are nearby.

Because the wage appears on both sides (through income), the simulator guesses the wages, recalculates them, and repeats until they stop changing, before moving any workers.

Key assumptions

  1. Only skilled workers move, and the share who are mobile is fixed. All other workers, and their spending, stay where they are, and better transport does not make anyone more mobile.
  2. One trade cost per link, based on rail journey times and standing in for all the costs of trading goods and services between two regions, including business travel.
  3. Congestion stands in for housing and commuting costs. There is no explicit housing market.
  4. Each region is treated as a single point, so commuting between nearby cities, such as Manchester and Leeds, is not captured.
  5. No knowledge spillovers, global connections or government, so the model leaves out several reasons firms stay in London.

How well does the calibrated model fit?

Even at the best-fitting value of θ, the model's “Today” equilibrium understates London's lead. This is itself informative, because it suggests that roughly a sixth of London and the South East's actual share of high-skill jobs cannot be explained by market size and transport costs alone, and must instead reflect forces outside the model, such as London's role as a global financial centre and the seat of government, or the long shadow of history.

6Does the evidence agree?

The empirical literature broadly supports the model's findings, although the effects of transport improvements are more varied in practice. Matas, Raymond and Roig (2015) use travel-time data to construct a measure of ‘effective density’, finding that greater accessibility increases wages. This suggests that improved transport links can generate some of the benefits of agglomeration without requiring firms and workers to physically relocate. Similarly, Ghani, Goswami and Kerr (2016) find that improvements to India's Golden Quadrilateral highway network increased manufacturing activity in connected districts, with no comparable gains in more distant control districts. Both studies support the NPR-style scenario, where improved connections between northern regions increase their effective market size and strengthen agglomeration outside London.

However, evidence from high-speed rail suggests that improved connectivity does not necessarily distribute economic activity more evenly. Zheng et al. (2019) find that the effects of China's high-speed railway stations vary considerably, producing outcomes ranging from ‘cities of hope’ to ‘ghost towns’. This supports the model's prediction that transport improvements can concentrate rather than disperse economic activity. Furthermore, Peri (2005), using 1.5 million patents and their citations, finds that knowledge spillovers remain largely concentrated within their region of origin. This suggests that improved connectivity alone may be insufficient to generate agglomeration benefits without an existing local economic base. Overall, the evidence indicates that transport links are most beneficial to regions capable of exploiting improved market access, consistent with the model's finding that Yorkshire is particularly vulnerable to losing industry when connections to London improve.

7The answer

The calibrated model suggests that transport links can either reduce or deepen regional inequality, depending on the regions they connect. Cutting journey times to London to HS2's planned 1h11 and 1h21 increases London's share of footloose industry from 61% to 70%, while Yorkshire's falls from 12% to 3%. In contrast, reducing Manchester–Leeds journey times to NPR's planned 33 minutes lowers London's share to 58%. These findings hold across all parameters tested, suggesting that links to London reinforce agglomeration in the core, whereas links within the North help reduce regional inequality.

However, combining both links produces uncertain outcomes, with London's share ranging from 46% to 73%. Even in the central case, northern gains concentrate in Manchester at Leeds' expense, showing that integration can create regional winners and losers. Furthermore, greater industrial concentration in London does not necessarily reduce northern living standards, as cheaper access to London's goods slightly increases real incomes across all regions.

The model also understates London's actual share of high-skill jobs (61% versus 73%), suggesting that factors beyond the model contribute to its dominance, consistent with Martin's (1999) criticism of New Economic Geography. Results also depend on congestion costs, highlighting the importance of housing and land markets. Ultimately, the model identifies potential mechanisms rather than precise forecasts, demonstrating that transport investment must be considered as a wider network to avoid deepening regional inequality.
